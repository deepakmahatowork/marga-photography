As twilight settles over the high sub-alpine pastures of Dhorpatan, the daily sounds of cattle bells and wind in the pines give way to a primal cadence that has resonated across these valleys since before recorded history: the steady, thunderous pulse of the *Dhyangro* drum.

In the secluded villages of the Kham Magar people in Western Nepal, the *Dhami-Jhankri* is neither performer nor relic. They are doctors of the unseen, mediums between the physical realm of high mountains and the spiritual world of ancestral forces (*pitri*) and nature spirits (*ban jhankri*).

To photograph a night *Chinta* ritual is to enter an entirely different perceptual space. Lit only by the glowing embers of a hearth fire and flickering butter lamps, the elder shaman dons the *Phephur*—a majestic crown crafted from pheasant and peacock feathers—and a heavy harness of hand-cast bronze bells and river cowries. As the tempo builds, the shaman's voice shifts into archaic tongues, chanting verses passed mouth-to-ear through dozens of generations.

Our camera sensors register what human eyes struggle to resolve in the gloom: the kinetic swirl of feathers, sweat glistening over painted clay talismans, and the transcendent focus in an apprentice's eyes as they strike the rhythm that carries their mentor across the threshold. In Dhorpatan, photography is not about capturing an exotic spectacle; it is about honoring the profound resilience of indigenous spiritual stewardship in the high Himalayas.
